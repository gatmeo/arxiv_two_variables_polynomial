\subsection{Partition and Standard Young Tableau}

Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$. A \vocab{partition} of $n$ is a weakly decreasing sequence of positive integers $\lambda=(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots ,\lambda_k)$ such that $\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i=n$.
One can define a paritial order on the set of paritions of $n$ called \vocab{dominance order}, defined by
\[
  \lambda\leq \mu \iff \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i\leq \sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_i \ \forall\ k\geq 1.
\]

Partitions could be visualized by their Young diagrams. The \vocab{Young diagram} of $\lambda$ consists of $n$ boxes that are left-aligned and bottom-aligned, such that the $i$-th row (labeled from the bottom) contains $\lambda_i$ boxes.
The \vocab{conjugate} of $\lambda$ is the parition whose Young diagram is the Young diagram of $\lambda$ reflected about the line $y=x$.
A \vocab{standard Young tableau} of shape $\lambda$ is obtained by filling the boxes in the Young diagram of $\lambda$ with $1, 2, \dots, n$ without repetition, such that the entries increase from left to right and from bottom to top. Denote by $\mathcal{Y}_\lambda$ the set of standard Young tableaux associated with $\lambda$.

\subsection{Robinson-Schensted Correspondence}

For any finite group $G$, we have $|G| = \sum_i (\dim V_i)^2$, where $V_i$ ranges over all the irreducible representations of $G$.
For $G=S_n$, it can be shown that its irreducible representations are labeled by partitions $\lambda$ of $n$, with dimension $|\mathcal{Y}_\lambda|$. Hence
\[
  \sum_{\lambda}^{}|\mathcal{Y}_\lambda|^2=|S_n|.
\]
This implies that there should be a bijection between $S_n$ and the set of pairs of standard Young tableaux associated with the same parition of $n$. Robinson and Schensted \cite{robinson1938representations, schensted1961longest} independently gave such a correspondence.

Define a map
\[
  \mathrm{RS}: S_n \to \bigsqcup_\lambda \mathcal{Y}_\lambda \times \mathcal{Y}_\lambda, \quad w\mapsto (P_w,Q_w)
\]
where $P_w$ and $Q_w$ are called the $P$-symbol and the $Q$-symbol of $w$ respectively.

Write $w$ in one line notation $w_1\cdots w_n$, i.e. $w_i$ is the value that $i$ maps to under $w$.
The $P$-symbol is constructed inductively. Start with $P_{w,1}$ a (not necessarily standard) Young tableau of $(1)$ with entry $w_1$. We inductively "insert" a box with entry $w_i$ to $P_{w,i-1}$ as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Let $P_{w,i}=P_{w,i-1}$, $R$ be the first row of $P_{w,i}$ and $x=w_i$.
  \item While $x$ is less than some of the element of $R$, do the following:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Replace the smallest integer $y$ greater than $x$ with $x$ in $P_{w,i}$.
      \item Let $R$ be the next row above, and set $x=y$, and repeat (2a).
    \end{enumerate}
  \item Now $x$ is greater than all the elements of $R$, place $x$ at the end of $R$ in $P_{w,i}$.
\end{enumerate}

This algorithm gaurentees that $P_{w,i}$ is increasing from left to right, bottom to top. Hence $P_w:=P_{w,n}$ is a standard Young tableau.
\begin{remark}
  The length of the first row in $P_{w,n}$ is the longest increasing subsequence of $w$ in the one line notation.
\end{remark}

$Q$-symbol on the other hand is easier to construct. It records the changes of shapes of $P_{w,i}$. More precisely, by definition of $Q_w$ has the same shape as $P_w$ with $i$ filled according to the position of box added at the $i^\text{th}$ step in $P_{w,i}$.

\begin{example}[exp:]{}
  Let $w$ be the permutation with $2431$ being its one line notation. Then
  \begin{figure}[H]
      \centering
    \begin{subfigure}{.6\linewidth}
      \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c}
      $i$ & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
      \hline
      $w_i$ & 2 & 4 & 3 & 1 \\
      \hline
      $P_{w,i}$ & \begin{ytableau}\none\\\none\\2\end{ytableau} & \begin{ytableau}\none\\\none\\2&4\end{ytableau} & \begin{ytableau}\none\\4\\2&3\end{ytableau} & \begin{ytableau}4\\2\\1&3\end{ytableau}
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{$P_w:=P_{w,4}$}
    \end{subfigure}
    \begin{subfigure}{.3\linewidth}
      \centering
      \begin{ytableau}4\\3\\1&2\end{ytableau}
      \caption{$Q_w$}
    \end{subfigure}
  \end{figure}
\end{example}

We will state, without proof, that $\text{RS}$ is a bijection.
In fact, one can start with a pair of standard Young tableau $P$ and $Q$ of the same shape and reverse the above construction to get a permutation $w\in S_n$.

Moreover, Sch\"utzenberger showed that inverting $w$ swap the $P$-symbol and $Q$-symbol.
\begin{proposition}[pps:]{\cite{schutzenberger1963quelques}}
  Let $w\in S_n$. Write $\text{RS}(w)=(P_w,Q_w)$. Then
  $\text{RS}(w^{-1})=(Q_w,P_w)$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Kostka Polynomial}

When studying symmetric functions, it is well known that there are different basis labelled by paritions.
The two are of interest here are the shur functions $s_\lambda$ and monomial functions $m_\lambda$. It is known that they are both basis of the symmetric functions, and one may be interested in expressing one in terms of the other:
\[
  s_\lambda=\sum_{\mu\in \Partition}^{}K_{\lambda,\mu}m_\mu,
\]
where $K_{\lambda,\mu}$ is called the \vocab{Kostka number}.
One can prove that $K_{\lambda,\mu}$ has a combinatorial description with \vocab{semi-standard Young tableaux} (or SSYT). Let $\lambda,\mu$ be partitions of $n$. Instead of filling the Young diagram of $\lambda$ with $\CB{1,\dots ,n}$ as in the case of SYT, a SSYT of shape $\lambda$ and weight $\mu$ is a filling of the Young diagram of $\lambda$ with $\mu_1$ copies of $1$, $\mu_2$ copies of $2$, and so on such that the numbers \emph{increase} from left to right and \emph{strictly increase} from bottom to top.

When $\mu=(1^n)$, SSYT of shape $\lambda$ and weight $\mu$ recovers the standard Young tableau of $\lambda$.

\begin{example}[exp:ssyt]{}
  Let $\lambda=(3,2)$ and $\mu=\{2,2,1\}$. There are 2 SSYTs of shape $\lambda$ and weight $\mu$.
  \begin{figure}[H]
      \centering
      \begin{ytableau}2 &2\\1&1&3\end{ytableau}
      \qquad
      \begin{ytableau}2&3\\1&1&2\end{ytableau}
  \end{figure}
\end{example}

There is a $q$-analogue of the Kostka numbers, defined as
\[
  K_{\lambda,\mu}(q)=\sum_{T\textnormal{ SSYT of shape $\lambda$ and weight $\upsilon$ }}^{}q^{\text{stat}(T)},
\]
for some satistics $\text{stat}(T)$ of $T$.

One of the statistics is called $\text{charge}(T)$. Define the \vocab{reading word} $\text{read}(T)$ of $T$ by reading the entries in $T$ from left to right in the top row, then the second row, and so on. For example, the reading word of the first SSYT in \Cref{exp:ssyt} is $22113$. And let $\mu^T$ be the conjugate of $\mu$. Perform the following algorithm on $\text{read}(T)$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Start at the end of $\text{read}(T)$ and scan left for $1$, record the position $i_1$. Then start at $i_1$, and scan left for $2$, record the position $i_2$. Loop around and continue to scan left if you hit the start of $\text{read}(T)$. Keep finding the next integer untill you found $\mu^T_1$. Then you get a subword of $\text{read}(T)$ in entries $\CB{i_1,i_2,\dots ,i_{\mu^T_1}}$.
    Remove the subword from $\text{read}(T)$ and repeat the above process to get a subword of length $\mu^T_2$, and so on.
  \item Define $\text{charge}(T)$ to be the sum of charges of the subwords obtaind in the previous step. Treat each subword $w_i\in S_{\mu^T_i}$ as a permutation, define $\text{charge}(w)$ to be the sum of $m-i$ over all $i$ for which $i+1$ occurs after $i$ in the one line notation of $w$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}[exp:]{}
  For the first SSYT in \Cref{exp:ssyt}, the reading word is 22113. The two subwords are $213$ and $21$. $\text{charge}(213)=(3-2)=1$ and $\text{charge}(21)=0$. Hence $\text{charge}(22113)=1+0=1$.

  For the second SSYT in \Cref{exp:ssyt}, the reading word is $23112$. The two subwords are $231$ and $12$. $\text{charge}(231)=(3-2)=1$ and $\text{charge}(12)=(2-1)=1$. Hence $\text{charge}(22113)=1+1=2$.
\end{example}
